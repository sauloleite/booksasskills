# Integrating with the Development Team

The problem: an architect's calendar fills with overlapping meetings, leaving no time to guide, mentor and answer questions. Meetings are unavoidable; the lever is controlling them.

## Two kinds of meetings

| Type | Who controls it | Strategy |
|---|---|---|
| Imposed on you (someone else invites you) | Hardest to control | Qualify every invitation; attend only what needs you |
| Imposed by you (you call it) | Fully yours | Minimize, agenda-driven, scheduled around developer focus |

### Meetings others call: triage rules

1. Ask the organizer why you specifically are needed. If the answer is "to keep you informed," read the notes instead.
2. Request the agenda in advance and use it to decide whether to go.
3. Check whether only one agenda item needs you; join for that item and leave afterward.
4. Weigh the meeting against time you could spend unblocking developers.
5. **Shield the team.** If an invitation also targets developers or the tech lead (especially when you and the tech lead both are), go in their place. Your meeting load rises, but team productivity and their respect for you rise more.

### Meetings you call: rules

1. Keep them to the minimum. Ask whether the meeting is worth more than the work it interrupts.
2. If the purpose is to share information, send a written message instead.
3. Publish an agenda and hold to it; park topics that do not concern everyone present.
4. Schedule developer meetings at the edges of focus time: first thing in the morning, just after lunch, or near the end of the day.

## Protecting flow

Flow is the state of total absorption in a hard problem, when hours pass like minutes and creativity peaks (see Csikszentmihalyi's *Flow*). Treat developer flow as a resource to protect: avoid mid-morning and mid-afternoon interruptions, batch questions, and prefer asynchronous channels for non-urgent items.

## Presence and availability

- **Sit with the team** when on-site. A separate office signals you are special and not to be disturbed; a seat alongside the team signals you are part of it and approachable.
- If you cannot sit with them, **walk around and be visible.** An architect who is never seen (different floor, closed office) cannot guide anyone.
- **Block recurring slots** (morning, post-lunch, late day) for conversations, help, questions, coaching and mentoring. Developers notice and respect this.
- Extend the habit to other stakeholders: a casual stop at the operations lead's desk on a coffee run keeps the channel open.
- **Remote teams** make all of this harder. For patterns, the authors recommend Part 4 of Jacqui Read's *Communication Patterns*. Remote equivalents an agent can suggest: published open office hours, staying present in team channels, optional drop-in calls, and an explicit "ask me anything" thread.

## Weekly calendar audit (agent procedure)

For each meeting on the architect's calendar for the coming week:
1. Classify: imposed on me / imposed by me.
2. For imposed-on: is my presence required (decision needs me, my expertise is on the agenda)? If not, decline and request notes. If partially, attend only the relevant item.
3. Is a developer or tech lead also invited? If yes, offer to represent them.
4. For imposed-by: could a written update replace it? Does it have an agenda? Is it at a low-disruption time?
5. Confirm at least one protected block per day exists for team availability.

## Closing idea

The chapter ends by stressing that getting along with people is the decisive ingredient of success (the authors cite Theodore Roosevelt). Every technique above serves stronger collaborative relationships with developers and stakeholders.
