## Why keep interview skills sharp

Interviewing is a career-long skill, not a one-off hurdle. It matters for a first job, an internal promotion, an external move, and the layoffs that are common in the industry. Even if content where you are, stay in practice. Your network helps here too: contacts share company knowledge, make introductions and serve as references.

## Preparation: research

Two passes:
1. **Before applying:** check whether the company and role fit your goals and values. This filters out poor matches.
2. **After an interview is booked:** go deeper on culture, stack, business challenges and the interview format. A little of this already separates you from candidates mass-applying.

Sources:
- LinkedIn: company background, culture hints, interviewer profiles, and possibly a shared connection who can help.
- Your network: insider views on process, team dynamics, even individual interviewers.
- Manual legwork: talk to current or former employees.
- **Interview style:** find out whether to expect mixed technical and behavioral questions, FAANG-style algorithm puzzles, or a take-home project. Knowing the format lets you prepare the right muscles.

Showing your research during the conversation signals seriousness and can influence both the offer decision and the package.

## Using AI for research, safely

Deep-research features in modern assistants can synthesize news, engineering blogs, financial reports and employee reviews into a briefing that goes well beyond a job post.

A prompt outline (my own wording) an agent can adapt:
```
Prepare me for a <role> interview at <company>. Give:
- origin, mission, leadership
- last 6-12 months: launches, acquisitions, pivots
- known stack, OSS work, eng-blog themes, likely tech pain points
- competitors and differentiators
- culture signals: reviews, remote policy, growth paths
- reported interview stages and question types
- 5 questions I could ask that show I did homework
Flag the 3 facts most worth mentioning. Cite sources.
```
Then drill down with follow-ups on the most relevant areas.

Guardrails:
- Verify facts before relying on them; models can be wrong or stale.
- Do not hand in AI-written cover letters or resumes wholesale. Recruiters spot them, and this is your first impression. Use AI for spelling, grammar and tightening, while keeping your own voice and specifics.

## Evaluating mutual fit

You are assessing them as much as they are assessing you. Turn research into questions that show interest and test fit:
- Team dynamics and culture
- Hard technical problems and how decisions get made
- Stack and development practices
- Growth and mentorship
- Travel expectations and time on the road

A useful closer: ask the interviewer where they would see you in a few years. It invites them to picture you already on the team.

## Common questions

**"Why are you leaving?"** Be honest and professional. Acceptable answers: better pay, fresh technical challenges, growth. Never criticize the current employer; it reflects on you, and professional circles are small enough that even anonymized stories can be recognized.

**The "N years from now" question.** They are probing ambition, commitment and alignment with company direction. Talk about growth and impact (technical leadership, architecture, mentoring) at a realistic pace. Your backward milestone plan is the ideal source.

## Technical questions to rehearse

| Prompt type | What it tests | How to answer well |
|---|---|---|
| Diagnosing a production performance problem | Systematic debugging | Narrate steps from symptom to hypothesis to measurement to fix |
| A hard problem you solved | Reasoning | Emphasize how you thought, not just the result |
| How you keep current and choose what to learn | Proactive growth | Name real sources and your evaluation criteria (your radar and filter) |
| Your approach to code review and feedback | Collaboration and quality | Cover both the technical checks and the interpersonal side |
| Design a small system (URL shortener, chat) | Architecture, data modeling, scale | Start from requirements, then walk through choices and trade-offs |

Mine the wins log for stories: defects squashed, early deliveries, measurable improvements. Review it before every interview.

## Mock interviews (human and AI)

Mock interviews are among the most effective prep tools. They make the real thing feel familiar so your genuine self shows.

**Partner choice:** someone who has sat on the interviewer side and will give concrete critique, not applause. Look in your workplace, network, local or online communities, or structured platforms such as interviewing.io.

**AI first, humans next.** Chatbots can generate role-specific questions and coding challenges, and voice mode lets you speak answers and field follow-ups. They are available anytime, good for early reps and for practicing thinking on your feet, but do not fully replace human feedback.

**Treat practice like the real thing:**
- Record sessions (with consent) and review them.
- Watch both what you say and nonverbal cues.
- Ask for targeted feedback on clarity and depth of technical explanations.
- Answer behavioral questions with concrete examples from your experience.
- Practice steady posture and appropriate eye contact.

## During the interview

**Own the technical round.** Walk in expecting to do well because you prepared. On a coding problem, do not start typing immediately. Clarify requirements and constraints, sketch the approach out loud, then implement. Interviewers usually care more about your reasoning than syntax. On unfamiliar problems: decompose, consider alternatives, explain the chosen route, and connect to relevant past wins.

## Professional presentation and virtual setup

General prep:
- Learn interviewers' names and roles.
- Prepare questions on things the job description omits.
- Have several resume copies, even virtually, for your own reference.
- Dress appropriately, including on camera.
- Sleep well; silence notifications.
- In person: arrive early to absorb delays and settle your mind.

Virtual extras:
- Test camera, mic, lighting and the meeting app login ahead of time.
- Silence every app; close heavy programs before live coding.
- Tidy visible background.
- Tell household members you need uninterrupted time.

**Mindset:** interviews are about finding a mutual match. Missing the first one, or the first three, is normal. Treat each as practice and as a chance to make a connection; a good impression can lead to later openings. Show real enthusiasm.

## After the interview

Two outcomes: offer or no offer. With an offer, evaluate it (use the five factors and life-phase weighting), negotiate, and prepare to start.

**Rejection is common and usually not about ability.** Reasons include an internal candidate, shifting priorities, a hiring freeze, or a closer skill match. Do not take it personally.

**Ask for feedback.** Instead of ruminating, request the reasoning. Feed any gaps into your radar and learning plan for the next attempt; there will be a next one.

**Keep the door open.** Another role may open, an interviewer may refer you elsewhere or move companies and remember you. Never burn the bridge.

## Pitfalls

- Generic applications with no research.
- Trusting unverified AI output or sending AI-generated cover letters.
- Jumping straight into code without clarifying.
- Badmouthing a current or past employer.
- Practicing only with friends who just say "great job."
- Treating rejection as a personal verdict and going silent.
