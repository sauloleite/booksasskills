# Soft Skills for Software Engineers (FSE Chapter 13)

Purpose: help a developer invest in the skills with the longest shelf life. Frameworks and APIs expire within a few years; people change on a scale of millennia, so communication, influence, and time management pay off across a 30 to 50 year career. The chapter treats these as habits that can be learned through deliberate repetition, not as personality traits. Introverts can excel; they just need to plan for recharging.

## Key decision rules (apply without opening references)

Communication channels
- Choose the channel deliberately along a warm-to-cold, high-touch-to-low-touch continuum. Ask: does this need a durable record? What channel does this audience actually read? How is information already flowing here?
- Anything with organizational risk (for example a critical vulnerability requiring an upgrade) needs a written, traceable trail, not just a hallway chat.
- Messaging: good for quick asynchronous questions, heads-ups, and offloading details; respect focus time and notification status; reply promptly even if only to defer; assume every message is logged and retrievable; avoid sarcasm because tone does not survive text.
- Use the Golden Rule. Promotions depend heavily on how others perceive you when you are absent.

Meetings
- Attending: if your role is unclear, ask the organizer (or your manager) what you are there for, whether you need the full slot, and whether an email would do. For high-stakes meetings with senior leaders, learn the unwritten rules from regulars, bring an ally, read materials beforehand, identify the decision maker, watch nonverbal cues, and confirm who is on the call.
- Running: agenda always, minimum audience, a note taker, a duration shorter than the 60-minute default (25/50 minutes or an offset start), start on time, give time back when done, park tangents, draw quiet people in, and send review material with enough lead time.
- A costly meeting can still be worth it when it prevents disruption and is well run.

Presentations and code
- Keep a one-minute pitch ready; short talks are harder, so lead with the point. Tailor 5 to 10 minute updates to the audience.
- Improvement comes only from volume and rehearsal (dry runs, test audiences, user groups, Toastmasters). Talks evolve with each delivery.
- Code is the most current documentation: optimize for the human reader (naming, concision, no cleverness, tests).

Garbled messages and audience
- When an "enterprise operator" rumor surfaces ("X is the standard", "we are out of compliance"), follow four steps: recognize it, find where the message broke, identify who is involved (including unknown stakeholders), then run a retrospective. Ask for evidence.
- With non-technical audiences, drop jargon and translate into business outcomes; "cool technology" never sells. Avoid condescension toward leadership.

Influence
- Prefer the subtle approach (others adopt the idea as theirs) over command; bullying backfires.
- Map the decision maker's priorities (time to market, cost, efficiency, calmer releases) and frame your proposal as serving them; cite the sources they already trust.
- Listen first, start small and prove it, recruit allies, influence the influencers, and let a trusted, tenured messenger carry the idea when you lack clout. Know your reputation; ask if unsure.

Stakeholders (power/interest grid)
- High power, high interest: engage proactively, adapt to their style, keep them updated in their preferred format; silence is risky.
- High power, low interest: transparent, self-serve information; watch for a sudden jump in interest.
- Low power, high interest: cultivate them as advocates, testers, interviewees; ignoring them can turn them against you.
- Low power, low interest: minimal effort.
- Hidden "level 2" players ("the VP won't accept that") may need to be brought into the room.

Time
- Guard your own calendar: block focus time, align hard work with your peak hours, do rote work in low-energy slots.
- Engineers need a maker's schedule (half-day blocks); one badly placed 30-minute meeting can wreck a morning. Cluster meetings, use do-not-disturb, propose meeting-free days as an experiment.
- Reserve thinking time up front; misconception is the costliest defect source and preventing a bug beats fixing it in production.
- Use structure for focus: Pomodoro (25 minutes on, short break, longer break after four rounds), capture stray thoughts instead of acting on them, and treat persistent resistance as a hint the task may be deferrable.

## Default workflow

1. Classify the request: communication channel, meeting, presentation, misinformation, influence, stakeholder, or time/focus.
2. Apply the matching rules above; for depth, checklists, or worked examples, open the reference file listed below.
3. Make advice concrete for the user's context: who the audience or decision maker is, what they value, and what record or cadence is needed.
4. Recommend one small, testable habit change and a way to check whether it worked after a few weeks.
