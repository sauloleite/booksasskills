## Principles

Time cannot be stored, scaled, or bought back, and demands never stop: meetings, reviews, incidents, administrative forms. You are the only reliable guardian of your calendar.
- Block uninterrupted time for the most important work. Many calendar tools support focus blocks and core hours.
- Know your energy curve. Put demanding work where you are sharpest (morning, early afternoon, whatever fits) and save routine tasks for low-energy periods. Stay flexible and set realistic expectations.

## Thinking time prevents defects

Defects found in production are the most expensive kind, which is why teams invest in automated tests and QA. Cheaper still is never writing the bug. Rich Hickey's "Hammock-Driven Development" talk argues that many serious software problems are misconceptions: teams start building before they understand the problem. Overloaded terms that look like synonyms but differ in critical ways are a frequent source. Reserve calendar time to reason through a design and its consequences before coding. Slowing down at the start saves time overall.

## Maker's schedule versus manager's schedule

Paul Graham's essay distinguishes two ways of using time:
- Manager's schedule: the day is a grid of interchangeable one-hour slots. Dropping a meeting into any open slot costs little. It suits consensus building and budget work.
- Maker's schedule: the useful unit is half a day or more. Software resembles a craft and needs it.

Why interruptions hurt engineers: work depends on loading a mental model of the system into your head. In debugging, you set breakpoints, reproduce, and track internal state; an interruption collapses that structure and rebuilding it takes a long time. A single 30-minute meeting mid-morning can split the morning into fragments too small to start anything hard.

## Protecting focus

- Turn off interruptions: close email and chat when you cannot resist them, tune notifications, and use do-not-disturb.
- Restructure the week: concentrate meetings into one or two days to free contiguous blocks.
- Advocate for meeting-free days or hours. If the organization has none, propose a time-boxed experiment and measure it.
- If someone wants your time, offer a slot at a natural boundary (start or end of day, next to an existing meeting) rather than mid-block.

## Staying on task

Email floods, chat, and the internet compete for attention. Struggling to focus on a task can be a signal that it can wait, but more often what helps is structure: a plan lowers cognitive load and turns focus into a habit.

Pomodoro technique (Agile at the scale of minutes):
1. Choose one task.
2. Work for 25 minutes on a timer.
3. When an unrelated thought arrives, write it down and continue.
4. At the bell, take a short break.
5. After four cycles, take a longer break.

Other aids: structured goal-setting programs such as the Focus Course, and habit-formation work such as James Clear's. Material from outside computer science is worth reading.

## Chapter wrap-up

Soft skills are rarely what engineers learn first, yet strong communicators tend to get promoted, the right technique at the right moment moves goals forward, and good time management keeps you steady when everyone else is scrambling. Passion for technology should not crowd out skills that last a whole career.

## Practice plan

Pick one or two items per month and review the effect afterward:
- Volunteer for a talk: first to your own team, then a local meetup.
- Put recurring focus and thinking blocks on your calendar and defend them for a month.
- Next meeting you organize: written agenda, minimum invite list, a non-60-minute length, and a note taker. Compare outcomes with your usual meetings.
- Run Pomodoro for two or three weeks; count completed cycles and judge whether output improved.
- Before each email, ask whether chat, a call, a ticket, or a document would serve better.
- Introduce one new tool or practice to your team using the influence template: start small, find an ally, frame it around a priority your lead already has.
- Map the stakeholders of your current project on the power/interest grid and pick an update format for each high-power person.
