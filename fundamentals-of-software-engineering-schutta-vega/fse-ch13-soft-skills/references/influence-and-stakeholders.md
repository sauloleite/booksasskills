## Why influence matters

Influence means getting people to choose what you want, ideally believing they reached it on their own. Unless you founded the company or run it, you cannot simply order people around (command-and-control rarely works, a theme from earlier chapters on leadership). Opportunities range from choosing a framework to choosing break-room snacks, and most of the time you are not the final decision maker.

## Finding the real influencers

The org chart does not reveal who carries weight. Clues: who keeps getting the most interesting assignments; whom senior leaders consult. Good managers know where their expertise ends and keep trusted experts on call. If you cannot reach the CTO, reach someone the CTO listens to. Influencing influencers is highly effective.

## Understanding and articulating value

1. State the benefits of your option in terms the other person cares about.
2. Find common ground.
3. Identify the decision maker's mission: time to market, developer productivity, cost reduction, release stability. Show how your proposal advances it rather than fighting it. Example: a leader tired of chaotic releases will respond to an argument about how your approach makes releases uneventful.
4. Match organizational context: startups usually prize getting features to customers faster; established firms under budget pressure usually prize savings.
5. Learn the decision maker's information diet (a favored analyst firm, a trusted vendor's publications) and cite those sources.

## Strategic approaches

Two archetypes for changing minds:
- The hammer: issue a directive. Rarely works with people who disagree.
- The ninja: nudge until they see the idea as their own. Usually works better.

Tactics:
- Avoid escalating volume or pressure; bullying nearly always backfires. Have a conversation instead.
- Expect it to be slow. Changing someone's mind takes patience and persistence.
- Start with a small pilot, demonstrate results, then expand.
- Listen. Enthusiasm can come across as aggression. Learn their concerns and what resonates, and sometimes simply rephrase in their terms.
- Manage your reputation. It speaks for you in rooms you are not in. If you do not know what it is, ask, even if the answer stings.
- Recruit allies who share your view; they add weight and spot angles you missed.

## The messenger matters

Sometimes the right move is to have someone else deliver your idea. A newcomer pushing a practice (say, test-driven development) may be politely ignored, while a long-tenured, well-liked colleague presenting nearly the same material, backed by visible results on their own team's code, wins broad support. Questions to ask:
- Whom will the decision maker consult?
- Is that person aligned with you?
- Can you work through someone with more standing?

It is less gratifying than taking the stage yourself, but it is often far more effective.

## Stakeholder management and the power/interest grid

Every project has many interested parties: teammates, management, end users, partner teams. Some are obvious (the CTO sponsoring a flagship initiative); others are hidden. "Level 2" players are not directly involved but can help or sink the project; you sense them in remarks like "the VP will never approve that." Even the idea of an absent person can block progress, and you cannot negotiate with someone who is not present, so sometimes the fix is to get them into the conversation.

Place each stakeholder on two axes, power and interest, and invest accordingly:

| Quadrant | Strategy |
|---|---|
| High power, high interest (manage closely) | Proactive, frequent engagement. Agree explicitly on how to work together and adapt to their style. Keep them informed in the format they prefer: demos, short summaries, one-on-ones. They can make or break the project and weigh heavily in promotions and bonuses; busy calendars make them hard to reach, and they may appear late and overturn decisions, so silence is dangerous. |
| High power, low interest (keep satisfied) | Give them peace of mind with transparent, easy-to-find information they can consult on their own schedule. Respect their time. Watch for a sudden switch to high interest. |
| Low power, high interest (keep engaged) | Treat as allies: their enthusiasm spreads, they volunteer to test features, allow shadowing, sit for interviews, and may have ties to powerful stakeholders. Neglect them and they may work against you. |
| Low power, low interest (monitor) | Minimal effort. |

### Worked example (invented)

Project: replacing an internal reporting system.
- CFO (high power, high interest): biweekly demo plus a one-page status; agree on decision checkpoints.
- Head of infrastructure (high power, low interest): a public status page and a short note at milestones.
- Finance analysts who use reports daily (low power, high interest): beta testers, interviewees, champions.
- Facilities team (low, low): informed only if the rollout touches them.
- Hidden player: the compliance lead invoked in "compliance will never allow cloud storage." Invite them to an early design review rather than arguing about them in absentia.

## Pitfalls

- Arguing on technical merit alone when the decision maker cares about cost or speed.
- Pushing harder when meeting resistance instead of listening.
- Insisting on being the face of the idea when a trusted peer would land it.
- Ignoring enthusiastic low-power users, or going quiet with powerful, interested sponsors.
- Letting an invisible "level 2" objection stall the project without engaging that person.
