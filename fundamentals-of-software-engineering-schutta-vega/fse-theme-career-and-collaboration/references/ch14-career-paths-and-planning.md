## Discovering what energizes you

Coding is the base of a technical career, but long-term satisfaction comes from knowing which kind of work lights you up. Interests also drift over a career; a plan built on what you loved at 22 should be revisited.

**Explore domains.** Each area brings its own problems, stakes and culture. Candidates worth sampling early: mass-market consumer apps, internal enterprise systems, analytics and data-heavy products, infrastructure and DevOps, games, edtech, healthcare, e-commerce, mobile, and AI/ML. Rough heuristics: if direct customer impact motivates you, lean consumer; a lifelong gaming passion points at interactive work. When unsure, the only reliable method is trying several.

**Use side projects as cheap experiments.** Day jobs often pin juniors to one domain and one stack. A side project removes that constraint: you pick the problem and the tools. A backend developer curious about UI should build something where polish matters. Two selection tactics:
- Market signal: gather postings from roughly ten employers you would like to join, tally recurring technologies, and favor the frequent ones.
- Deliberate contrast: also pick a language, runtime or platform unlike your day job. Contrast widens your model of how software works.

Projects that scratch your own itch (a personal site, a blog that teaches what you learned) are easier to sustain and often expose you to tools your employer never would. Public side work and open source contributions are optional but help you stand out in internal or external applications.

**Energy journal.** While experimenting, record each activity with +1 (energizing) or -1 (draining) and a one-line reason. Prompt questions: Which problems make you eager to start? UI or data and algorithms? Does hunting bugs in tangled systems excite or exhaust you? Creative design or precise backend logic? Review after a few weeks for clusters.

Example journal lines:
```
2026-03-02 +1 wiring a websocket feed; loved seeing live updates
2026-03-04 -1 nudging CSS margins for an hour; tedious
2026-03-09 +1 profiling slow SQL; satisfying detective work
```
Pattern here: data and performance energize, visual polish drains.

## The menu of career tracks

Early on it is easy to assume the job is "write better code forever." In reality the field branches widely. Stay flexible while holding a general heading that fits your strengths.

**Reversibility rule:** essentially the only career moves you cannot undo are serious ethical breaches and wrecked relationships with colleagues. Stacks, employers and tracks can all be redirected with planning.

### Technical expert (individual contributor) track
Lets you keep advancing while staying hands-on. Rewards depth, system design and technical leadership. Ladders commonly run senior, staff, principal, then sometimes distinguished or fellow; naming varies by company.

| Role | Core work | Signals of fit |
|---|---|---|
| Architect | Shapes system structure, makes high-level technology choices, sets standards, turns business needs into technical designs | Enjoys trade-off reasoning, can explain tech to non-technical people, stays curious; typically 8+ years in; still codes to keep designs grounded and keep credibility |
| Staff / principal / tech lead | Solves hard problems while steering direction beyond one team | Wants breadth of influence without leaving code; mentors; deep domain expertise; senior pay can equal or beat managers |

### Leadership track
Rewards strategy, people skills and tying engineering to business goals. Typical ladder: team lead, then director, VP and eventually C-suite.

| Role | Core work | Signals of fit |
|---|---|---|
| Engineering manager | Builds teams, removes blockers, owns delivery and people growth | Cares about people and process; accepts writing little or no code; balances team morale with business targets; pay often weighted toward bonus and equity |
| Technical product manager | Owns product vision and priorities, links stakeholders and engineers | Enjoys business plus technology; more strategy, less coding; a stepping stone toward senior product or strategy positions |

### Hybrid and alternative tracks

| Role | Core work | Trade-offs |
|---|---|---|
| Developer advocate | Content, talks, community building; carries user feedback back to product | Needs teaching and speaking skill, travel, constant trend tracking; leads to DevRel or technical marketing |
| Sales engineer | Demos, answers technical objections, builds buyer trust | Presentation and people skills plus depth; travel to clients and trade shows; leads to sales leadership or customer success |
| Technical entrepreneur | Spots a market gap and builds a business around it | High risk, high potential upside; needs broad tech and business knowledge; often begins as a side project alongside a job |

Others include SRE, DevOps and many niche hybrids.

## Self-advocacy moves

- Talk to practitioners in roles you are curious about; a coffee or lunch invitation raises the odds of a yes.
- Tell your manager your goal explicitly. A manager cannot sponsor a goal they have never heard.
- Ask about formal or informal shadowing, or a temporary rotation into another team.
- You own your progression; nobody else is obligated to steer it.

## Backward milestone planning

Engineers decompose big problems; apply the same move to a career. Fix a destination 3 to 5 years out (architect, engineering manager, client-facing product role), then define intermediate steps and work backward.

Worked example (my own, for a goal of engineering manager):

- **Years 5+:** run a team end to end, own hiring decisions, manage a budget, partner with product on roadmap.
- **Years 3-5:** act as tech lead on a small team, run retrospectives and planning, mentor two juniors, handle one cross-team escalation, take a facilitation or feedback course.
- **Years 1-2:** become solid in the current stack, volunteer for onboarding new hires, ask the current manager for monthly career one-on-ones, shadow a hiring interview.

The chapter's own example targets architect: early years emphasize mastering the stack, harder problems and starting system design while meeting architects periodically; middle years add leading projects, mentoring, distributed systems, scalability and helping an architect with deliverables; later years add owning major architectural decisions, cross-team standards, wider influence and presentation and meeting-leadership skills.

Rules for a good plan: milestones are concrete and checkable; each band includes a non-technical skill; one milestone per band involves a person already in the target role.

## Life phases and the five job factors

Career decisions sit inside a life. Score any role on five factors:

1. **Compensation**: salary, benefits, equity. Fairly paid?
2. **Team and culture**: do you like the people and the organization?
3. **Work content**: do you like the actual problems?
4. **Growth and prospects**: will this set up the next role? What is the opportunity cost of the experiences and contacts you forgo?
5. **Work-life balance**: can you keep your relationships and health intact?

Typical reweighting by phase:

| Phase | Usually rises in priority | Typical choice |
|---|---|---|
| Early career | Growth, challenge | Accept lower pay at a fast-moving startup for learning |
| Family formation | Health coverage, steady hours, predictable income | Established employer |
| Mid-career | Compensation | Strategic moves between companies |
| Later career | Balance, culture, legacy | Mentoring, meaningful or lasting projects |

These are tendencies; always ask the user rather than assume.

## Company archetypes

| Type | Upside | Downside |
|---|---|---|
| Startup (new venture on a novel product) | Fast, varied learning; many hats | Long hours, high failure risk, thin benefits |
| Traditional company (tech supports a non-tech core business) | Stability, regular hours, good benefits and balance | Slower pace, older tech, lower total pay |
| Big tech | Financial security, benefits, resume credibility, cutting-edge work | Very selective hiring; heavy algorithm prep |
| Consulting (firm or freelance) | Exposure to many clients, stacks and industries; often higher pay; freelancers choose projects | Constant adaptation, possible travel; freelancers must find clients and run a business |

## Practical fit checklist

When options are otherwise close, these questions often tip the decision:
- Do you need the employer's health coverage, or is a partner's plan available?
- How much do you value staying in one place?
- Can you absorb the uncertainty of equity instead of cash?
- Is there enough time and energy in your life right now for a steep ramp-up?
- More travel or less?
- Will it build your personal brand?
- Remote or office, and is the commute acceptable?
- What is your risk tolerance?
- Does it give the balance you need?
- Does the domain or product interest you enough to learn it?

There is no universally correct path, only the right one for current circumstances.

## Planning with slack

Aim high but leave room for life events. Plans rarely unfold as written, and detours are often where the best growth happens. Prefer steady, sustainable progress over an aggressive timeline that ends in burnout. Remember to notice and enjoy progress along the way rather than only the destination.

## Practice suggestions

Turn the chapter into a short set of actions an agent can propose:
1. Draft a personal radar with entries in all four rings.
2. Identify one person whose career you admire and invite them for a coffee conversation about how they got there.
3. Write goals at one, three and five years.
4. Join a local meetup, ideally one that gathers in person on a regular schedule.
5. Open a wins log today.
6. Start writing about problems you solve, even privately; explaining forces a second, deeper round of understanding.
7. Run a voice-mode AI mock interview for a specific target role, then ask for critique.
8. Book an hour-long mock interview with a peer or mentor, record it with consent, and review.
9. Choose a dream employer and build a prep doc: stack, recent news, and three to five questions you would ask.
